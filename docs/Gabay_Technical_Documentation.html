Please read this first

Gabay is a working proof of concept, tested only in supervised sessions at a desk. It is not wearable yet (the Raspberry Pi sends pictures to a PC, and the PC plays the sound). Nobody should rely on it to walk safely. This document reports what was actually measured and says "not measured" where no measurement exists. A "pass" in the scorecard means a number was measured and met its target, nothing more.

1. Project overview and purpose

The problem

A white cane finds what is at the end of the cane: the floor, a table leg, a step. It does not warn about a chair back at chest height, a person walking toward you, or a pole beside the path. Gabay is meant to add a second layer of awareness that tells a blind person, in plain spoken words, what is close and in which direction.

The idea in one paragraph

Gabay is a small bar worn on the chest. It has two cameras side by side, the way two eyes are side by side. Because the two cameras see the same scene from slightly different positions, the system can work out how far away each thing is. This is called stereo vision. The bar also has a motion sensor (to know if it is tilting or turning fast) and a compass. A small computer on the bar (a Raspberry Pi) sends everything to a stronger PC. The PC calculates the distances, decides whether something is in the way on the left, ahead, or on the right, and plays a short voice message such as "ahead, about one metre".

Design principles

  • Unknown is never "clear". If the system cannot see (a blank wall, glass, darkness), it says "can't see ahead" instead of staying quiet.
  • Say when something is wrong. Late or missing data is announced: "tracking delayed", "Gabai stopped, cable or camera lost", "reduced accuracy".
  • One voice at a time. A single priority queue decides what is spoken. Urgent messages cut in; repeats are suppressed.
  • No speech engine while walking. Voice messages are recorded in advance, so playback is instant and predictable.
  • Every decision can be explained. Each frame has a trace of timings, checks and the reason a message was or was not spoken.

4. System architecture and data flow

4.1 Two machines, one job each

The Raspberry Pi is a sensor box. It only captures and sends. The PC does all the maths and decisions and plays the sound. This keeps the wearable part small and cool, and lets a powerful graphics card run the depth model.

4.2 Main flowchart: from input to output

Read this top to bottom. Green boxes run on the Raspberry Pi, blue boxes on the PC's processor, purple on the PC's graphics card, and dashed boxes are optional or advisory.

4.3 What happens to one picture, step by step

  1. Capture (Pi). Two reader threads grab the left and right camera. A "pair" is formed when the two capture times are close. The frame is labelled with its Pi capture time in milliseconds, and the same number appears in Pi logs and PC logs, so the two can be matched.
  2. Send (Pi). The JPEG pair, gyro and accelerometer samples, compass readings and a health message (CPU temperature, power flags, free memory) go through one TCP socket, protected by a shared token. If the link drops, the sender reconnects by itself.
  3. Receive (PC). The receiver decodes the message, keeps only the newest pair (older unread pairs are counted as overwritten), and logs gaps and decoding errors.
  4. Orientation (PC). Gyro and gravity (and compass if enabled) give camera tilt. The tilt matters because a tilted camera sees the floor differently.
  5. Rectify. The saved calibration straightens both pictures so that a point appears on the same row in both. This makes matching possible.
  6. Depth (GPU). LAS2 measures how far each pixel shifts between the left and right picture. This shift is called disparity.
  7. Trust check. A second pass checks left→right and right→left agreement. Pixels that disagree are marked "unknown" and are never treated as free space.
  8. Zones. Disparity becomes distance in metres. The floor is removed using the known camera height and tilt. What remains is split into left, ahead, right. The nearest obstacle in each band is rounded to the nearest half metre. Special results: drop_ahead and cant_see_ahead.
  9. Alert logic. Priority, repeat suppression and stale-data rules decide whether to speak.
  10. Speak. One of 23 clips is played. The log stores the reason for every message that was not spoken.
  11. Log. Each frame has a trace with stage timings and checks. A telemetry web page (port 8000) and a CSV file record the same data for later graphs.

4.4 How the alert is chosen

4.5 Clocks and delay

The Pi and the PC have different clocks, and the code never subtracts one from the other. Every message carries the Pi's own capture time. The PC estimates the offset using PING/PONG round trips and reports the uncertainty (about half the round-trip time). The delay between the camera and the IMU (measured: 22 ms) is stored once and removed from camera timestamps before use.

5. How each part works (plain language)

5.1 Seeing distance with two cameras

Hold a finger in front of your face and close one eye, then the other. The finger seems to jump sideways. The closer the finger, the bigger the jump. Gabay measures that jump (the disparity, in pixels) for every part of the picture. Distance then follows from a simple formula:

distance Z (metres) = focal length f (pixels) × baseline B (metres) / disparity d (pixels)

With this rig: f ≈ 620–645 px, B ≈ 0.042 m  →  f×B ≈ 26 px·m
Example: disparity 26 px  →  1 m.   Disparity 13 px  →  2 m.   Disparity 8.7 px  →  3 m.

Two things follow. Close things are measured well (large disparity). Far things are measured poorly, because a tiny matching error of half a pixel becomes a big error in metres:

The depth model is a neural network (Lite Any Stereo V2). It looks at both pictures and predicts disparity for every pixel, including smooth areas where simple matching fails. It is a prediction, not a measurement, so the system adds independent checks (left-right agreement) before trusting a pixel.

5.2 Zones and floor removal

Looking forward, the floor itself appears in the depth image. The floor must not be reported as an obstacle, so the software computes where the floor should be for the known camera height and the tilt from the IMU, and removes it. What is left standing above the floor is an obstacle. The picture is divided into three vertical bands (left, ahead, right) and the nearest obstacle in each band is reported. If the floor ahead is missing, the result is drop_ahead. If too few pixels in the middle have trusted depth, the result is cant_see_ahead.

Depends on camera height

The floor model needs the camera's height above the floor (--cam-h). A wrong height produces false alarms, for example "drop ahead" on a flat floor. This is tested on purpose in the simulator (clear_floor_wrong_height) and the logs record the height the system believed.

The spoken distance is the distance along the camera's line of sight, not along the ground. With the camera tilted 35° downward the walking distance is about 0.15 m shorter than the spoken value; the spoken value has not been corrected for this.

5.3 The alert system

Alerts come from a fixed set of 23 voice clips made once by make_alert_audio.py using the Windows voice:

GroupClipsPriority
Direction + distanceleft, ahead, right × 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, 3.0 m = 18 clips. Spoken like "ahead, about one metre".3 if within 1 m, 4 if 1–3 m

Reduced accuracy"reduced accuracy" (fast head turn)2
System failure"tracking delayed", "Gabai stopped, cable or camera lost"1 (highest)

Rules (constants in gabai/alerts.py): a higher-priority alert cuts in over a lower one for 2 seconds; the same alert is not repeated within 3 seconds unless the obstacle got closer; a frame older than 300 ms triggers "tracking delayed"; no data for 1 second triggers "Gabai stopped", repeated every 5 seconds.

5.4 Safety and health monitoring

  • Link monitor watches frame age (stale after 300 ms, dead after 1 s).
  • Fast-turn flag. The gyro gives turn speed. Steady: under 25°/s. Turning: 25–60°/s. Fast: over 60°/s, when the system says "reduced accuracy". The reason: the two cameras are not triggered at the same instant (see sync), so fast turns smear depth.
  • Self-check. py -m gabai.tools.selfcheck --deep verifies calibration, files and models before a hardware session.
  • Thermal governor. When the GPU is hot it switches off optional extras (the near-range second pass, the single-camera veto), but never the zone check or alerts.

5.5 Tracking and mapping (built, not yet driving alerts)

The system can also work out how the camera moves (visual-inertial odometry with Basalt, plus its own stereo odometry), build a 3D map from many frames, clean it by averaging many views of the same 5 cm cube, and save it. A relocaliser can recognise a saved place. These parts are tested on recordings and in a simulator and are described in Other planned work.

5.6 Hands and objects (built, tested on video only)

MediaPipe finds 21 points on each hand, which are lifted to 3D using the depth map. Rules decide when a hand has grabbed an object (closed hand near an object, then moving away with it) and released it (open, slow hand for 0.3 s). Object memory stores where things were put down in an SQLite file so "where are my keys?" can later be answered. None of this is connected to the live alert queue yet.

5.7 Logging and diagnostics

Every run writes a JSON-lines log (logs/<run>/gabai.jsonl), with bounded size, rotation and automatic clean-up. Raw camera footage is never saved unless asked. Per-frame CSV logs hold latency, depth model time, nearest distance per zone, the alert, capture-to-speech time and the Pi's health. The report tool turns these into the tables and graphs below.

6.2 Sensor calibration

  • Gyro: noise about 0.07–0.10 °/s per axis; the Pi measures start-up bias for 3 seconds (repeated up to 5 times if the rig moved).
  • Accelerometer: 6-orientation calibration. Gravity error on the six calibration poses fell from 0.13 m/s² to near zero. The raw board read about 0.95 g before calibration.
  • Compass: hard-iron and soft-iron correction by tumbling the rig through every direction. The raw field was about 89 µT against an expected 25–65 µT, meaning a magnetic part sits close to the sensor. The compass was found to have been uncalibrated earlier, which is why heading drifted; the tool and tests were added, but a final calibration quality number is not recorded in the project files.
  • Camera ↔ IMU: measured delay 22 ms (range 2–32 ms), match quality 0.82, rotation matrix stored in data/imu_sync.json.

6.3 Left/right camera timing

The two webcams cannot be triggered at the same moment; the software pairs frames that arrive close together. Because the cameras top out at 30 fps, the worst case is about half a frame period (17 ms).

Reading these numbers

Expected error at distance (calculated, not measured)

10. Current capabilities and limitations

What the system can do now

  • Capture two synchronised camera streams plus gyro, accelerometer and compass on a Pi and send them to a PC over Wi-Fi, with automatic reconnect.
  • Produce a depth map at about 14.5 fps with a close-range accuracy of about 0.5 %.
  • Divide the view into left / ahead / right and speak the nearest obstacle's direction and distance within 3 m.
  • Warn about a drop ahead, say "can't see ahead" when depth is missing, and announce late or lost data and fast turns.
  • Record every run, show a live telemetry page, produce graphs and an HTML report, and explain why any alert was or was not spoken.
  • Record sequences and run Basalt tracking on them; build, clean, save and re-find 3D maps (on recordings and in simulation).
  • Detect hands, apply grab/release rules and store object memory (tested on video and simulation).

11. Future development: the mobile app

The planned next step is an Android phone app that receives Gabay's alerts and plays the sounds, so that the user hears warnings in their earphones through the phone they already carry, instead of from the PC's speaker. The project abstract already specifies the app, and the existing code has a natural place for it to plug in.

11.1 Goals

  • Deliver each alert to the user's ears with low delay, through wired, Bluetooth or bone-conduction earphones.
  • Keep working with the screen off and the phone in a pocket.
  • Give a helper or caregiver a simple screen showing link health, recent alerts and battery.
  • Stay safe: if the connection fails, the phone itself must say so.

11.2 Architecture with the app

11.3 How the integration would work

  1. Where it plugs in. The PC's AlertSpeaker class already accepts a replaceable player function (AlertSpeaker(clips_dir, player=...)). Today that function plays a WAV file on the PC. A new phone bridge would be another player: instead of playing the sound, it sends the alert's key and distance to the phone. No change to depth, zones or the alert rules is needed.
  2. Send the alert, not the audio. A message is only a few dozen bytes, for example: 
    { "t_capture": 1234.567,   // Pi capture time (the one system clock)
  "seq": 4821,             // counter, to spot lost or repeated messages
  "key": "ahead",          // left | ahead | right | drop_ahead | cant_see_ahead | ...
  "distance_m": 1.0,       // already rounded to 0.5 m
  "priority": 3,           // 1 system failure ... 4 obstacle 1-3 m
  "expires_at": 1235.067 } // drop it if it arrives too late
    The phone already holds all 23 voice clips (the same recordings), so playback is instant and does not depend on bandwidth.
  3. Transport. A persistent WebSocket (or plain TCP, as the Pi link already uses) over the same Wi-Fi network or a phone hotspot. The phone connects out to the PC, like the Pi does, with a shared token. Tailscale can be used when not on the same network.
  4. Heartbeat and stale rules. The PC sends a heartbeat every second. If the phone sees nothing for 1 second it plays "Gabai stopped" itself and repeats every 5 seconds, exactly like the PC rule. The phone also drops any alert whose expires_at has passed, so an old warning is never spoken late.
  5. Local priority queue. The phone repeats the same queue rules (priority, repeat suppression) so it behaves safely even if two messages arrive together after a Wi-Fi hiccup.
  6. Acknowledgements. The phone replies with seq numbers and its play time, so the PC log can measure the real "capture to ear" delay, closing the gap noted in the M3 test.
  7. Fallback. If the phone is not connected, the PC speaker keeps working (dashed arrow in the diagram).

Risks to plan for
  • Bluetooth audio delay can be 100–200 ms. Wired or low-latency headsets are better for urgent alerts; the measured end-to-end delay must include it.
  • Wi-Fi is already the weakest link. The app adds a second wireless hop (PC → phone). A phone hotspot or a wired/USB tether lowers the risk.
  • Battery and thermal limits of a phone running a foreground service for hours.
  • Android background restrictions may stop the service; battery-optimisation exemptions must be requested.
  • The app does not remove the PC dependency. Fully wearable use still needs the compute moved off the PC (see roadmap).

13. Glossary

TermMeaning
Stereo visionWorking out distance from two pictures taken from slightly different positions.
BaselineThe distance between the two cameras (about 4.2 cm here).
DisparityHow many pixels an object shifts between the left and right picture. Bigger shift = closer.
RectificationStraightening both pictures so matching points lie on the same row.
CalibrationMeasuring the cameras' lenses and relative position using a checkerboard.
IMUInertial measurement unit: gyroscope (turning) + accelerometer (gravity, movement). Here, the BMI160.
LatencyDelay between something happening and the system reacting.
p50 / p95Median, and the value 95 % of measurements are below.
LAS2Lite Any Stereo V2, the neural network that predicts depth.
SLAM / VIOTracking the camera's movement and building a map. Basalt is the tracker used.
VoxelA small 3D cube (5 cm) in a map.
Under-voltageThe Pi's supply drops below 4.63 V, which can cause throttling and dropouts.
Foreground serviceAn Android component that keeps running visibly (with a notification) when the app is not on screen.
